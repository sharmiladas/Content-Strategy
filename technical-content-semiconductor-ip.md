## The Problem

In semiconductor design automation, technical content isn't a nice-to-have — it's the interface between deeply complex IP and the engineers who have to implement it correctly the first time. At a semiconductor design automation company, I owned content strategy for exactly that: API references and release notes for a design automation platform, spanning multiple product versions and customer tiers, where an inaccurate reference document can cost a customer real engineering time.

## Building the Team and the Content Operation

I hired and led a technical content team of 12 engineers and writers, responsible for both user documentation and marketing collateral for the platform's IP group — an unusual pairing, but a deliberate one. Technical accuracy and go-to-market content were treated as one content operation rather than two disconnected functions, so the same domain expertise informed both.

Day to day, that meant:

- Managing the release calendar, version control, and cross-functional review cycles across multiple concurrent product versions and customer tiers
- Developing content assets across the full range a semiconductor customer actually needs — datasheets, feature briefs, whitepapers, user guides, and web tutorials
- Building and managing sales enablement material — datasheets, whitepapers, case studies, and technical documentation — so sales conversations were backed by the same accurate technical content engineers relied on

## Research-Driven Content Strategy

Technical writing in this space can't just react to what engineering ships — it has to anticipate what customers will need and where the content itself creates risk or opportunity. I drove market research, gap analyses, and competitor research to identify where content was helping or hurting adoption, and used those findings to prioritize what got built next.

I also defined the end-to-end journeys and experience requirements for technical content — mapping what a customer actually needs at each stage of evaluating, implementing, and troubleshooting the platform, rather than organizing content around internal product structure. That mapping directly shaped later initiatives: audience and task analysis, information reuse across document types, and clearer visual communication of inherently technical information.

## Impact

- Led a 12-person technical content team across concurrent product versions and customer tiers
- Unified technical documentation and sales enablement content under one strategy, backed by the same domain accuracy
- Used market and competitive research to prioritize content investment, rather than producing content reactively

## Tools

Release management tooling, version control, competitive and market research, information architecture
