# Content as a Business Asset

## The situation

A global content organization supported customers across many products, languages, and channels. Teams were productive, but the work was still largely discussed in terms of output: pages published, requests completed, and releases supported. That made it difficult to explain where content changed a customer outcome or deserved further investment.

## My role

I led a global team of content strategists, content engineers, and localization specialists. The work covered governance, information architecture, platform improvements, quality, localization, and measurement.

## What changed

We connected content decisions to the customer journey rather than treating every request as an isolated deliverable. The operating model included:

- A governance group for cross-product decisions and unresolved ownership questions
- Content inventories and audits focused on gaps, duplication, and lifecycle risk
- Taxonomy and information-architecture work to improve discovery and personalization
- A move toward structured, reusable content delivered across several channels
- Shared quality guidance and a measurement program using behavioral data, surveys, and experiments

The phrase “content as a business asset” became useful because it changed the questions leaders asked. Instead of asking only how much content a team produced, we could discuss where content reduced support demand, improved task success, or lowered operating cost.

## Results

The program was associated with:

- A 15% reduction in support cases
- A 27% reduction in vendor costs
- A 10% improvement in customer satisfaction
- A 17% increase in weekly sessions

The larger result was a clearer way to decide what to fund, reuse, improve, or retire.

## What I learned

Measurement does not make every content decision obvious. It does make tradeoffs easier to discuss. Customer outcomes and team effort need to sit beside each other; optimizing one while ignoring the other creates a fragile system.
